## Example 1 Text:

Although social movement research has clearly shown the importance of political opportunities and external structures in constraining and motivating movements, there has also been a lot of conflicting results on when and how opportunities matter. The readings this week all attempted to better specify the effects of political opportunity in new or old context. Although some of the results are context dependent, their general findings provide guidance for how the research that follows can better test for the effects of political opportunity.  I will begin by elaborating on the current understanding of political opportunity and how the readings for this week have built upon it. Next, I will expand on this understanding by proposing how movement outcomes affect their relationship with systemic opportunities and how future research might better investigate this. And finally, I will pay particular attention to the unique character of democratizing movements, and how to more rigorously understand their perception of opportunities. 

As many scholars acknowledge, political opportunity has been generally elaborated in the context of advanced democratic states. From this research, the standard understand of political opportunity has been elaborate, in that there is a relationship between traditional access to the polity and the mobilization of non-traditional collective action such as protest. More specifically, a curvilinear relationship is said to exist, where mobilization is most intense in systems with a mix of open and closed access, while mobilization is comparatively reduced in fully open or fully closed system. Meyer and Minkoff (2004) builds off of this by questioning how previous work has conceptualized opportunity and its relationship with mobilization. They introduce several important distinctions. First, there are differences between changes in the system that objectively open it, and the perception of that system as being more or less open to challenge. Second, that these opportunities can either be specific to a movement or applicable to all movements. And third, that different types of movement activities might respond differently to opportunities. Meyer and Minkoff (2004) establish the importance of these distinctions through an examination of the civil rights movement, demonstrating how all these factors are important in understanding protest mobilization, organization founding and movement outcomes. 

Unlike Meyer and Minkoff (2004) which refine political opportunity structure in the context of an advanced democracy, other works have attempted to translate it into authoritarian or democratizing regimes. Kurzman (1996) and Inclán (2008) both examine single movements in these types of regimes and both find that theories of political opportunity are lacking in explaining mobilization. Kurzman (1996) finds that the movement actors behind the Iranian Revolution did not mobilize in response to opening of opportunities within the state but because of perceptions of the strength of the opposition. The movement actually mobilized in spite of a pervasive perception that the state was ready to crackdown at any movement. Similarly, Inclán (2008) finds that the Zapatista movement was most mobilized in areas with the least opportunity and that opportunities had a tendency to decrease the amount of protests. Almeida (2003) does not focus on a single movement, but on general protest waves in El Salvador that encompassed a cross-section of leftist groups and issues. Almeida (2003) finds a complex relationship between the order of opportunities and threats, with increasing opportunity creating a set of organizations that were then radicalized into violent protests when opportunities were limited and new threats imposed. In all these cases then, traditional theories of political opportunity seem to be limited or incorrect in explaining mobilization.

The work of Meyer and Minkoff (2004) though help to provide an answer to connect these diverse results. In proposing that movements might respond both to general openness and issue specific openness Meyer and Minkoff seem to assume that all movements would respond similar to general openness. Just as it is important to differentiate between types of openness, it is also important to differentiate types of movements by the goals that they pursue. The movements presented by Kurzman (1996) and Inclán (2008) are both focused on opening the state. For these movements, the structural opportunities that are traditionally examined become the grievances that fuel the movement, and later the success of the movement. This is important as researchers expect a different relationship between movement success and mobilization than between openings to the system and mobilization. Under this conceptualization, the Zapatistas should be expected to demobilize in states where they have been relatively successful in opening the state. Although this overlap between success and openness is most acute for democratization movements, it is also present in less transformational movements. For example, Meyer and Minkoff (2004) find that the Civil Rights Movement deescalated in response to a variety of issue specific openings. These openings can also be conceptualized as movement outcomes though. Research needs to also acknowledge the difference in opportunities that pre-date movement mobilization and how opportunities change over movement trajectory. As demonstrated by Almeida (2003), the timing can be very important. 
Synthesizing the above indicates that future research needs to pay particular attention to the goals of a social movement and be careful when outcomes overlap with forms of political opportunity. This problem is most acute for democratizing movements, but even in other types of movements there might be overlap. In order to better identify how opportunity and outcomes overlap, research should examine a variety of movements in the context of a single system. This work can begin by repeating Meyer and Minkoff’s (2004) analysis with other movements that were happening in a similar time frame, for example the women’s movement or the peace movement. This will allow researchers to identify how a variety of movements with a variety of outcomes response to general changes in opportunity. This work should also break down the effects of opportunity into different periods of movement growth. Analysis should examine the opportunities that are present at initial mobilization, at the movement’s peak, and when a movement begins to deescalate. In order to do this, analysis will likely have to focus on the difference in mobilization across regions in a single county (similar to the analysis in Inclán [2008]).  

By connecting opportunity and outcomes, a political opportunity framework becomes ineffective in understanding mobilization of democratization movements as their goals and opportunities almost perfectly overlap. Instead, it might be better to use the framework of perceived opportunities proposed by Kurzman (1996). Before this can be done though perceived opportunities must be more rigorously defined. Meyer and Minkoff (2004) incorporate a more rigorous version of perceptions, by examining how the system can send signals to movement actors, but this captures only a portion of the potential signals that activists might be watching.  Movement activists might also watching for signals from international actors who would support the movement, or from other movement actors that indicate the strength of the movement. Without a clear conception of what activists are paying attention to it is not possible for researchers to theorize on how their actions might be affected by changes. At the same time, research similar to Kurzman’s (1996), which focuses on accounts of the movement after its success are at risk of activist’s post hoc rationalization.  One way to untangle this is to begin a longitudinal survey of movement activists in developing democracies. This survey would focus on how leaders and organizers perceive the structure of a variety of opportunities that might be available to them. Although the data might not be immediately useful to research, by capturing the trend of the perception of opportunities prior to increase in mobilization research can see where opportunities matter. This work would have a variety of barriers to overcome including the difficulties in surveying individuals in states without strong protections for political organizations. Some of these limitations could be overcome by targeting activists with international connections, although this would not be without a tradeoff in the validity of the survey. It is important though as works like Kurzman (1996) and even Meyer and Minkoff (2004) demonstrate that it is not just how the state responds to activists but how activists expect the state to respond. 

Political opportunity has been heavily critiqued for potentially becoming a “sponge that soaks up every aspect of the social movement environment” (Gamson and Meyer 1996: 275). The works presented here though provide direction in how to better conceptualize political opportunity so that it can remain a useful tool for social movement researchers. Future work must acknowledge not only the differences in opportunity between different movements but also the differences in outcomes and how opportunity and outcomes can interact. At the same time work needs to pay particular attention to how movement actors perceive their relationship with the state. Together both of these paths will allow work to identify when and how exactly opportunities present themselves.

Quiz

Instructions: Select the best answer for each question.

1. According to the text, what is the primary limitation of traditional political opportunity theories when applied to democratizing movements?
    - A. They overemphasize the role of perceived threats rather than actual structural openings
    - B. They assume that structural opportunities and movement goals are distinct, when they often overlap in democratization contexts
    - C. They do not sufficiently distinguish between different types of protest activities within a movement
    - D. They fail to account for the influence of international actors on movement mobilization

2. How does Meyer and Minkoff (2004) advance the conceptualization of political opportunity beyond previous research?
    - A. By distinguishing between objective system changes, perceived openness, and movement-specific versus general opportunities
    - B. By arguing that political opportunity should be measured exclusively through longitudinal surveys
    - C. By proving that curvilinear relationships between access and mobilization do not exist in any context
    - D. By demonstrating that opportunities only matter in advanced democratic states

3. Based on Inclán's (2008) study of the Zapatista movement, which of the following findings challenges traditional political opportunity theories?
    - A. The movement primarily responded to signals from international actors rather than domestic conditions
    - B. The movement mobilized most intensely in areas with the least political opportunity
    - C. The movement's success was directly proportional to increased state openness
    - D. The movement deescalated when faced with new threats from the state

4. What methodological approach does the author propose for future research to better understand the relationship between political opportunity and movement outcomes?
    - A. Conducting case studies of single movements in isolation to ensure depth of analysis
    - B. Focusing exclusively on the post-hoc accounts of movement leaders after their success
    - C. Developing quantitative models that measure only objective structural changes in the state
    - D. Examining multiple movements within a single system across different periods of their trajectory

5. According to the text, what is a key risk in relying solely on research like Kurzman's (1996) that focuses on movement accounts after success?
    - A. Such research cannot account for the role of international support in movement mobilization
    - B. It is at risk of capturing activists' post-hoc rationalization rather than their actual decision-making processes
    - C. It fails to distinguish between different types of opportunities available to the movement
    - D. It overemphasizes the importance of perceived threats rather than actual structural conditions
